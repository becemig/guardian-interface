# Blue Light Toxicity and Artificial Light at Night

## Abstract

Artificial light at night ALAN — the pervasive exposure to blue-enriched light from screens, LED lighting, and urban light pollution during the evening and nighttime hours when the human circadian system is adapted to darkness — is now recognized as a major environmental health hazard producing circadian disruption, melatonin suppression, sleep disruption, metabolic dysregulation, immune impairment, and increased cancer risk through the melanopsin-mediated activation of the alerting and phase-delaying pathways of the circadian system at precisely the time when the body requires the transition to darkness to initiate the restorative nocturnal physiological program. Melanopsin suppression by screens — the acute suppression of pineal melatonin secretion by blue-enriched screen light at intensities as low as 10 lux, far below the illuminance of most indoor environments — is mediated by the high sensitivity of ipRGCs to 480 nm blue wavelength light, with the sustained nature of melanopsin phototransduction meaning that even moderate screen exposure in the two hours before sleep produces melatonin suppression and circadian phase delay sufficient to impair sleep quality and duration. Circadian disruption pathophysiology — the downstream health consequences of chronic ALAN exposure including metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, cardiovascular disease, depression, immune impairment, and cancer — is mediated through multiple mechanisms: melatonin suppression reduces its anti-proliferative and antioxidant effects, mistimed feeding driven by extended wakefulness produces metabolic dysregulation, impaired sleep reduces immune function and cognitive consolidation, and chronic circadian misalignment between the central SCN clock and peripheral organ clocks produces the metabolic and immune dysregulation of social jetlag. ALAN epidemiology — the population-level evidence linking nighttime light exposure to health outcomes — demonstrates that night shift workers have significantly elevated risks of breast cancer, colorectal cancer, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease, with the International Agency for Research on Cancer IARC classifying shift work involving circadian disruption as a probable human carcinogen in 2007. Blue light blocking strategies — amber-tinted glasses filtering wavelengths below 530 nm, dim red lighting in the evening, and screen night mode settings — have been demonstrated in randomized trials to attenuate melatonin suppression, improve sleep quality, and reduce circadian phase delay when used in the two hours before sleep. The Evening Yin descent disruption correspondence in TCM describes the classical understanding that the evening is the time of Yin ascendance and Yang descent, with the body requiring decreasing stimulation and increasing stillness to allow the Yin to consolidate and the Shen to descend into restful sleep — a process directly disrupted by the alerting and Yang-activating effects of blue light exposure after sunset.

## Melatonin Suppression Mechanism

- ipRGC melanopsin maximally sensitive to 480 nm blue light — screen spectral peak.
- Suppression threshold: as low as 10 lux blue-enriched light — typical screen brightness.
- Sustained suppression: melanopsin adaptation slow — 2 hours exposure delays clock 1-3 hours.
- Dose-response: intensity duration and wavelength all determine suppression magnitude.

## Circadian Disruption Consequences

- Social jetlag: mismatch between biological clock and social schedule — widespread in modern life.
- Metabolic dysregulation: mistimed food intake during biological night impairs insulin sensitivity.
- Immune impairment: melatonin suppression reduces NK cell activity and anti-tumor surveillance.
- Depression: circadian misalignment is a primary driver of seasonal and non-seasonal depression.

## ALAN Epidemiology

- Night shift workers: 30-50 percent elevated breast cancer risk — IARC probable carcinogen.
- Outdoor light at night: satellite-measured ALAN correlates with obesity rates across countries.
- Indoor light at night during sleep: even dim light during sleep impairs glucose metabolism.
- Blue light blocking glasses: RCT evidence for improved sleep and mood with evening use.
