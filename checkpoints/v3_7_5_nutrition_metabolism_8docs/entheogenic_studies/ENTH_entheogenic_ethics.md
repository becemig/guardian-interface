# Entheogenic Ethics

## Abstract

The rapid expansion of psychedelic therapy, ceremonial use, and commercial interest in entheogenic substances raises profound ethical questions about cultural appropriation, indigenous sovereignty, commercialization, and the responsibilities of non-indigenous practitioners and participants toward the indigenous communities who have stewarded these sacred medicines for thousands of years. Cultural appropriation in the entheogenic context occurs when non-indigenous practitioners adopt indigenous ceremonial forms, claim traditional knowledge, or use sacred medicines without acknowledgment of their origins, without reciprocal benefit to the originating communities, and without the deep cultural context that gives these practices their meaning and safety. Indigenous sovereignty over sacred medicines encompasses the right of indigenous communities to control access to their traditional knowledge, to determine who may participate in their ceremonies, to receive equitable benefit from the commercialization of knowledge derived from their traditions, and to have their intellectual and spiritual property rights recognized in law and practice. The ayahuasca tourism industry in Peru and Brazil — now generating hundreds of millions of dollars annually — illustrates both the potential and the risks of the entheogenic economy: it provides income for indigenous communities and creates genuine healing experiences for many participants, while also enabling exploitation of indigenous practitioners, commodification of sacred practices, and the extraction of traditional knowledge without adequate reciprocity. Peyote conservation represents an acute crisis — the sacred cactus of the Native American Church grows slowly and is severely threatened by overharvesting driven by expanding non-Native interest, raising urgent questions about the responsibilities of non-indigenous practitioners toward the plants themselves and the communities for whom they are irreplaceable. Reciprocity obligations for non-indigenous practitioners include financial support for indigenous-led organizations, advocacy for indigenous rights, active opposition to the criminalization of traditional plant medicine use, and the humility to acknowledge the limits of one's understanding of traditions one did not grow up within.

## Cultural Appropriation

- Appropriation occurs when sacred practices are adopted without acknowledgment, context, or reciprocity.
- The form without the cultural context can be ineffective, misleading, or actively harmful.
- Non-indigenous practitioners have an obligation to acknowledge the origins of their practices.
- This corresponds to the TCM principle of honoring the lineage from which one's knowledge derives.

## Indigenous Sovereignty

- Indigenous communities have the right to control access to their traditional knowledge and ceremonies.
- Intellectual property law has largely failed to protect indigenous knowledge from extraction and commercialization.
- Benefit-sharing agreements between pharmaceutical interests and indigenous communities remain rare and inadequate.
- Guardian Interface acknowledges that traditional knowledge documented here derives from living traditions deserving respect.

## Reciprocity Obligations

- Financial support for indigenous-led healing, cultural preservation, and rights organizations.
- Advocacy for decriminalization of traditional plant medicine use by indigenous practitioners.
- Purchasing from indigenous-owned suppliers and supporting indigenous economic sovereignty.
- Humility about the limits of non-indigenous understanding of traditions one did not grow up within.
