# Life History Theory

## Abstract

Life history theory — the evolutionary framework governing the allocation of finite biological resources among competing demands of somatic maintenance, growth, reproduction, and survival — provides the deepest evolutionary context for understanding constitutional health, aging, and the trade-offs that govern why organisms age, why some individuals age faster than others, and how early life experiences program the pace of biological aging across the lifespan. The central trade-off of life history theory is the allocation of metabolic resources between somatic maintenance — the repair, immune defense, and homeostatic processes that maintain the soma — and reproduction — the energy-expensive production of offspring — with investment in one necessarily reducing investment in the other. Fast versus slow life history strategies represent the two poles of the life history continuum: fast life history organisms mature early, reproduce early and often with limited parental investment, age rapidly, and die young — an optimal strategy in unpredictable high-mortality environments where delaying reproduction risks death before reproducing. Slow life history organisms mature late, reproduce late with high parental investment, maintain the soma for longer through superior repair and immune investment, and live longer — an optimal strategy in predictable low-mortality environments where investing in somatic maintenance pays dividends through extended reproductive opportunity. Developmental plasticity — the capacity of early life experience to program the organism's life history strategy in response to environmental cues — provides the mechanism by which childhood adversity, resource scarcity, and early life stress produce accelerated biological aging and fast life history phenotypes: early adversity signals a high-mortality environment in which fast reproduction is more adaptive than somatic maintenance, calibrating the organism's biology toward faster aging. Adversity calibration — the epigenetic programming of telomere length, inflammatory baseline, HPA reactivity, and reproductive timing by early life experience — produces the well-documented association between childhood adversity and accelerated biological aging. The Jing conservation longevity strategy in TCM describes the cultivation principle that conserving Kidney Jing through appropriate rest, moderate sexual activity, and avoidance of constitutional excess extends the lifespan — a direct correspondence to the slow life history somatic investment strategy.

## Somatic vs Reproductive Trade-off

- Finite resources must be allocated between somatic maintenance and reproduction.
- High somatic investment: slow aging, late reproduction, high parental investment, long lifespan.
- High reproductive investment: fast aging, early reproduction, low parental investment, short lifespan.
- The trade-off is mediated by IGF-1 mTOR signaling: high activity accelerates both growth and aging.

## Fast vs Slow Life History

- Fast: early maturation, early first reproduction, multiple offspring, low parental investment.
- Slow: late maturation, late first reproduction, few offspring, high parental investment.
- Environment calibrates strategy: high mortality unpredictable environment selects fast.
- Low mortality predictable environment selects slow — somatic investment pays off.

## Adversity Calibration

- Early life adversity signals high-mortality environment — calibrates toward fast life history.
- Epigenetic programming: telomere shortening, elevated inflammatory baseline, HPA hyperreactivity.
- Accelerated pubertal timing in adversity-exposed children — reproductive urgency signal.
- Accelerated biological aging measured by epigenetic clocks in adversity-exposed adults.
