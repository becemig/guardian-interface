# Philosophy of Science in Medicine

## Abstract

Philosophy of science in medicine — the systematic examination of the epistemological foundations, methodological assumptions, theoretical frameworks, and knowledge-production practices of biomedical science and clinical medicine — reveals that medicine operates within multiple competing philosophical frameworks including reductionism and systems thinking, employs methodological tools with known limitations including the randomized controlled trial, and undergoes periodic paradigm shifts as accumulating anomalies force revision of foundational assumptions, with Daoist philosophy offering a distinct epistemological ground that prioritizes relational knowing, process ontology, and the integration of observer and observed. Hypothetico-deductive method — the scientific method as formalized by Karl Popper in which scientific knowledge advances through the formulation of falsifiable hypotheses, the design of experiments capable of disconfirming those hypotheses, and the provisional acceptance of hypotheses that survive repeated attempts at falsification, with the demarcation criterion of falsifiability distinguishing scientific claims from metaphysical ones — provides the formal epistemological structure underlying evidence-based medicine while also highlighting its limitations in complex biological systems where confounding, emergence, and nonlinearity make clean falsification rare. Thomas Kuhn's paradigm shifts — the historical analysis of how scientific fields do not progress by gradual accumulation of facts but through discontinuous paradigm shifts in which the conceptual framework governing what counts as a problem, a solution, and a valid method is replaced wholesale when accumulating anomalies can no longer be accommodated within the existing paradigm — illuminates the historical transitions in medicine from humoral to cellular to molecular to systems paradigms and anticipates the current transition from reductionist molecular medicine to systems and complexity medicine. Reductionism versus systems thinking — the philosophical tension between the reductionist research program that has dominated twentieth century biomedicine, explaining biological phenomena by reducing them to molecular mechanisms, and the emerging systems biology framework that recognizes that the properties of complex biological systems cannot be predicted from the properties of their components because they arise from the interactions between components at multiple organizational levels, with emergence, nonlinearity, and self-organization as irreducible features of living systems. The Dao as epistemological ground correspondence in TCM describes the classical understanding that ultimate knowledge in Chinese medicine is not propositional knowledge about mechanisms but relational knowing of patterns — the physician knowing the patient as a dynamic process in relation to their environment, season, and constitution — an epistemological stance corresponding to modern complexity and phenomenological approaches to medical knowledge.

## Falsificationism in Medicine

- RCT as gold standard: attempts to falsify null hypothesis of no treatment effect.
- Limitations: cannot falsify complex interventions population level claims.
- Publication bias: failed falsifications rarely published — distorts knowledge base.
- Complexity problem: biological systems rarely produce clean falsifiable predictions.

## Paradigm Shifts in Medicine

- Humoral paradigm: four humors governing health through balance.
- Germ theory paradigm: specific pathogens causing specific diseases.
- Molecular paradigm: genes and molecules as fundamental explanatory level.
- Systems paradigm: networks interactions emergence as explanatory level.

## Reductionism vs Systems

- Reductionism: explain whole by reducing to parts — dominant biomedical approach.
- Emergence: system properties not predictable from parts — requires different methodology.
- Systems biology: networks pathways attractors as explanatory units.
- Complexity medicine: nonlinear dynamics chaos self-organization in physiology.
