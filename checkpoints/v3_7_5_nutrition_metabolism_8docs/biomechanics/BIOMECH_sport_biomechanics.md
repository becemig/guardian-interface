# Sport Biomechanics

## Abstract

Sport biomechanics applies the principles of mechanics to human athletic movement, revealing the fundamental relationships between force, velocity, power, and skill that underlie all athletic performance and providing the theoretical basis for training program design, technique optimization, and injury prevention. The force-velocity relationship is one of the most fundamental principles of muscle mechanics — as the velocity of muscle shortening increases, the force the muscle can produce decreases, and vice versa. This inverse relationship means that maximum power output occurs at intermediate velocities approximately one-third of maximum velocity — a principle that governs optimal loading in power training. The stretch-shortening cycle SSC is the most important biomechanical principle for athletic performance — it describes the sequence of eccentric loading followed immediately by concentric contraction that characterizes nearly all high-performance athletic movements including jumping, sprinting, throwing, and striking. The elastic energy stored in tendons and the stretch reflex activation of muscle spindles during the eccentric phase augment the subsequent concentric contraction producing greater force and power than a pure concentric contraction alone. Tendon stiffness is a critical determinant of SSC efficiency — stiffer tendons store and return elastic energy more rapidly, producing better reactive strength in fast SSC activities like sprinting, while more compliant tendons are better suited to slow SSC activities like heavy squatting. Rate of force development RFD — how quickly a muscle can develop force — is increasingly recognized as more important than maximum strength for athletic performance, as most athletic movements occur in time windows too brief to reach maximum force. Athletic Qi cultivation in the Chinese martial arts traditions — the development of Jin through standing post Zhan Zhuang, partner practice, and power training — corresponds to the biomechanical development of SSC efficiency, tendon stiffness, and RFD through progressive loading.

## Force-Velocity Relationship

- Maximum force at zero velocity: isometric strength — the ceiling of force production.
- Maximum velocity at zero force: unloaded speed — the ceiling of movement speed.
- Maximum power at intermediate velocity: the training sweet spot for power development.
- Power training must target the specific force-velocity zone of the athletic demand.

## Stretch-Shortening Cycle

- Eccentric phase: muscle and tendon loaded under stretch storing elastic energy and activating spindles.
- Amortization phase: the coupling between eccentric and concentric — shorter is more powerful.
- Concentric phase: stored elastic energy released augmenting contractile force.
- Plyometric training develops SSC efficiency by progressively loading the amortization phase.

## Tendon Stiffness

- Stiff tendons transmit force rapidly — essential for fast SSC activities like sprinting and jumping.
- Compliant tendons absorb force gradually — better for slow heavy loading activities.
- Tendon stiffness increases with progressive overload training — an adaptation to mechanical stress.
- Injury risk increases when tendon stiffness changes rapidly — either increasing or decreasing.
