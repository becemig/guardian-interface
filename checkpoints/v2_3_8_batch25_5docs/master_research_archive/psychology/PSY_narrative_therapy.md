# Narrative Therapy

## Abstract

Narrative therapy developed by Michael White and David Epston is a postmodern psychotherapy approach based on the premise that people organize their experience through stories and that problems arise when dominant problem-saturated stories eclipse alternative more resourceful stories. Externalizing the problem — separating the person from the problem through language — reduces shame and blame while creating space to examine the problem's influence and resist it. Re-authoring conversations identify unique outcomes — moments when the problem did not dominate — and use these as the foundation for building alternative preferred stories. Definitional ceremonies use an audience of witnesses who reflect back what they heard in the alternative story without interpretation or evaluation. The narrative metaphor proposes that identity is a story — a preferred account of who one is — that can be consciously revised through re-authoring practice. Shen story in TCM — the narrative that the Heart-Mind tells about itself and its experience — corresponds to the narrative therapy understanding of identity as a authored story subject to revision.

## Externalizing

- Externalizing separates the person from the problem through specific linguistic practices.
- The person is not depressed — they have a relationship with depression that can be examined and changed.
- Externalizing creates distance from the problem enabling reflection on its history, tactics, and effects.
- Questions explore the problem's influence: How has depression been affecting your relationship with yourself?

## Unique Outcomes

- Unique outcomes are moments when the dominant problem story did not apply — exceptions to the problem pattern.
- They are identified through careful questioning: When was there a time when depression did not have as much influence?
- Unique outcomes become the seeds of alternative preferred stories when elaborated through re-authoring.
- They demonstrate that the problem story is not total — there are already moments of the preferred story.

## Re-Authoring

- Re-authoring conversations develop unique outcomes into thick alternative stories with history and identity.
- The alternative story is elaborated across time — past, present, and projected future.
- Witnesses reflect back what they heard without interpretation enabling the person to hear their alternative story.
- The thickened alternative story becomes increasingly available as an identity resource against the problem story.
