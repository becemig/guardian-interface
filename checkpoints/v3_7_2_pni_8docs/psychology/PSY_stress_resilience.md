# Stress and Resilience

## Abstract

Stress is the physiological and psychological response to demands that exceed perceived coping resources. Hans Selye's general adaptation syndrome describes three stages of stress response — alarm, resistance, and exhaustion — corresponding to acute activation, adaptive coping, and resource depletion. Allostatic load is the cumulative physiological cost of chronic stress adaptation producing wear across multiple biological systems. Resilience is the capacity to adapt successfully in the face of adversity, trauma, and significant stress. Suzanne Kobasa's hardiness construct identifies commitment, control, and challenge as the personality factors that buffer stress and promote resilience. Hormesis describes the beneficial effects of moderate stress exposure that stimulate adaptive responses strengthening resilience capacity. Kidney Yang fortitude — the deep constitutional will and strength to meet adversity — provides the TCM constitutional basis of resilience.

## Selye's Stress Model

- The alarm phase activates the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system for immediate stress response.
- The resistance phase maintains elevated stress hormones enabling sustained coping with ongoing demands.
- The exhaustion phase represents resource depletion when demands chronically exceed recovery capacity.
- Modern chronic stress rarely allows the recovery phase producing cumulative allostatic load.

## Resilience Factors

- Social support is the most consistently documented resilience factor across cultures and populations.
- Meaning and purpose buffer stress by providing the motivational framework that makes adversity worthwhile.
- Self-efficacy — belief in one's capacity to influence outcomes — enables active coping rather than helplessness.
- Emotion regulation through mindfulness, exercise, and social connection supports resilience under stress.

## Hardiness

- Commitment corresponds to engagement with life and work as meaningful rather than alienated.
- Control corresponds to the belief that one's actions influence outcomes rather than passive helplessness.
- Challenge corresponds to viewing change and difficulty as opportunities for growth rather than threats.
- Hardy individuals show lower cortisol reactivity and faster recovery from acute stress exposure.
