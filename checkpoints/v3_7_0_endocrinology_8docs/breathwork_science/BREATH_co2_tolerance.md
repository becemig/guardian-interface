# CO2 Tolerance and Respiratory Physiology

## Abstract

Carbon dioxide — long dismissed as merely a metabolic waste product to be eliminated through exhalation — is now recognized as a master regulator of respiratory physiology, cardiovascular function, and tissue oxygen delivery, with CO2 tolerance emerging as one of the most clinically significant and trainable physiological parameters relevant to health, performance, and breathwork practice. The Bohr effect — discovered by Christian Bohr in 1904 — demonstrates that hemoglobin releases oxygen to tissues more readily in the presence of higher CO2 and lower pH, meaning that the efficiency of oxygen delivery to working tissues depends on maintaining adequate CO2 levels in the blood: paradoxically, breathing too much — hyperventilation — reduces CO2, causing hemoglobin to hold oxygen more tightly and actually reducing tissue oxygen delivery despite increased oxygen inhalation. The hypercapnic drive — the primary chemical stimulus for breathing in healthy individuals — responds to rising CO2 levels by increasing respiratory rate and depth, and the sensitivity of this drive is highly trainable: individuals with low CO2 tolerance feel air hunger at low CO2 levels and breathe excessively, while high CO2 tolerance individuals can maintain comfortable breathing at higher CO2 levels, producing more efficient respiration with lower respiratory rate and higher HRV. Nasal breathing — breathing through the nose rather than the mouth — is superior to mouth breathing in virtually every respiratory metric: the nose filters, humidifies, and warms inspired air; the nasal turbinates produce turbulent flow that slows air intake and increases CO2 absorption time; and the nasal sinuses produce nitric oxide that travels with inspired air into the lungs producing vasodilation and antimicrobial effects. The Buteyko method — developed by Ukrainian physician Konstantin Buteyko in the 1950s — is the most systematized clinical application of CO2 tolerance training, using controlled breath reduction exercises to raise the CO2 set point, reduce respiratory rate, and eliminate the overbreathing patterns that Buteyko proposed underlie asthma, anxiety, and numerous other conditions.

- Hyperventilation lowers CO2, causing hemoglobin to grip oxygen tighter — reduced tissue delivery.

## CO2 Tolerance Training

- BOLT score Body Oxygen Level Test measures CO2 tolerance: seconds of comfortable breath hold after normal exhale.
- Low BOLT score under 20 seconds: low CO2 tolerance, tendency to overbreathe, low HRV.
- High BOLT score over 40 seconds: high CO2 tolerance, efficient respiration, high HRV.
- Training through breath reduction exercises raises BOLT score and improves all respiratory metrics.
