# Microbiome-Ecosystem Correspondence

## Abstract

The microbiome-ecosystem correspondence — the recognition that the human gut microbiome is a miniature ecosystem whose health mirrors and depends upon the health of the larger environmental ecosystems in which the human body is embedded — provides one of the most profound bridges between ecological medicine and individual health, demonstrating that the biodiversity crisis of the natural world and the dysbiosis crisis of the human microbiome are not merely analogous but causally connected. The soil microbiome — the community of microorganisms inhabiting agricultural and natural soils — is the ecological foundation of human microbial exposure: the diversity and composition of the soil microbiome determines the microbial communities of the food we eat, the air we breathe, and the surfaces we touch, making soil health a primary determinant of human microbiome health. Mycobacterium vaccae — a soil bacterium with potent immunoregulatory and antidepressant effects, producing serotonin and anti-inflammatory cytokines when encountered through soil contact or inhalation — is a paradigmatic old friend whose modern absence through urbanization, indoor living, and chemical agriculture corresponds to the epidemic of depression and immune dysregulation in industrialized populations. The biodiversity hypothesis proposes that the loss of environmental microbial biodiversity through habitat destruction, pesticide use, and urbanization directly reduces the diversity of microbial exposures available to the human immune system, impairing immune education and producing the immune dysregulation of allergic and autoimmune disease. Traditional fermented foods — kimchi, sauerkraut, kefir, miso, natto, tempeh, yogurt, kombucha — represent the accumulated wisdom of traditional food cultures in maintaining gut microbiome diversity and function through probiotic food preparation traditions that predate the modern understanding of microbiology by millennia. The One Health framework — the recognition that human health, animal health, and ecosystem health are inseparable and must be addressed as an integrated system — provides the policy and research framework for addressing the microbiome-ecosystem correspondence at the societal level.
