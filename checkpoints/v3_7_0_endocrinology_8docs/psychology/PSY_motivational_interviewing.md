# Motivational Interviewing

## Abstract

Motivational Interviewing MI developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick is a collaborative, person-centered counseling style for eliciting and strengthening a person's own motivation for change through the exploration and resolution of ambivalence. MI emerged from Miller's clinical experience with problem drinkers finding that empathic listening and drawing out the person's own reasons for change was more effective than confrontation or advice-giving. The spirit of MI comprises four elements: partnership — a collaborative relationship between equals rather than expert-to-patient, acceptance — unconditional positive regard, affirmation, and autonomy support, compassion — prioritizing the client's welfare, and evocation — drawing out the client's own wisdom and motivation rather than installing it from outside. Ambivalence — simultaneously wanting and not wanting change — is the normal state for most people considering behavior change and MI works with rather than against this ambivalence. Change talk is the client's own statements favoring change — desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment, activation, and taking steps — and sustain talk represents arguments for the status quo. The fundamental MI skill is selectively reinforcing change talk while not reinforcing sustain talk without directly arguing for change. OARS — open questions, affirmations, reflective listening, and summarizing — are the core MI microskills. The righting reflex — the helper's instinct to correct, advise, and fix — is the primary obstacle to MI practice. Liver Qi activation in TCM — the arousal of the Liver's characteristic initiative, decisiveness, and forward movement — corresponds to the MI process of evoking the client's own intrinsic motivation for change.

## Spirit of MI

- Partnership treats the counseling relationship as collaborative between equals rather than expert prescribing.
- Acceptance includes unconditional positive regard, accurate empathy, affirmation, and autonomy support.
- Compassion actively promotes the client's welfare and prioritizes their needs over the helper's agenda.
- Evocation draws out the client's own motivation and wisdom rather than installing correct attitudes from outside.

## Ambivalence and Change Talk

- Ambivalence is the simultaneous desire for and resistance to change — the normal state of most change consideration.
- Change talk includes desire to change, ability to change, reasons for changing, and need to change.
- Commitment language, activation statements, and taking-steps talk indicate movement toward actual behavior change.
- Sustain talk represents the arguments for staying the same — reflecting the other side of ambivalence.

## OARS Microskills

- Open questions invite elaboration and exploration rather than yes-no responses.
- Affirmations recognize the person's strengths, efforts, and positive qualities genuinely and specifically.
- Reflective listening captures and reflects back the meaning and feeling of what the person has said.
- Summarizing collects and synthesizes what has been said linking change talk and preparing for next steps.
