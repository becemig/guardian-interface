# Kinetic Chains and Movement Patterns

## Abstract

The kinetic chain concept describes the body as a linked system of segments where force generated at one segment is transmitted through the chain to produce movement at another. Closed kinetic chain movements, where the distal segment is fixed, produce co-contraction and joint compression forces fundamentally different from open chain movements. Regional interdependence theory recognizes that dysfunction in one region of the body predictably produces compensatory dysfunction at distant sites, often through myofascial force transmission pathways. The joint-by-joint approach, developed by Cook and Boyle, describes the alternating mobility-stability requirements of joints from the foot to the skull, providing a framework for movement assessment and corrective exercise. Force transmission through fascia allows muscles to act on joints and segments they do not directly cross.

## Open vs Closed Kinetic Chains

- Open kinetic chain: distal segment free to move - limb exercises in non-weight-bearing
- Closed kinetic chain: distal segment fixed - weight-bearing exercises like squat and push-up
- Force direction: CKC produces joint compression - more stable and functional
- Muscle recruitment: CKC recruits multiple muscle groups simultaneously
- Proprioception: CKC exercises produce greater proprioceptive activation
- Rehabilitation: CKC preferred for joint rehabilitation - OKC for isolated strengthening

## Force Transmission Through Fascia

- Myofascial force transmission: muscles transmit force to non-adjacent structures via fascia
- Epimuscular pathways: lateral force transmission between adjacent muscles via shared fascia
- Chain loading: tension in one myofascial line loads all connected structures
- Clinical example: plantar fascia tension influences hamstring length via superficial back line
- Stiffness transfer: fascial restrictions in one segment increase load on remote segments
- Therapeutic implication: treating remote restriction resolves local pain

## Regional Interdependence

- Definition: dysfunction in one region produces predictable dysfunction in remote regions
- Hip-lumbar: limited hip mobility drives lumbar compensation - low back pain
- Ankle-knee: limited ankle dorsiflexion drives knee valgus in squat
- Thoracic-cervical: thoracic kyphosis drives cervical forward head posture
- Assessment: movement screening reveals compensation chains
- Treatment: correcting proximal dysfunction resolves distal symptoms

## Joint-by-Joint Approach

- Cook and Boyle model: joints alternate between mobility and stability requirements
- Foot: stability platform
- Ankle: mobility - dorsiflexion essential
- Knee: stability - resists valgus and varus
- Hip: mobility - multiplanar range of motion
- Lumbar: stability - resists excessive motion
- Thoracic: mobility - rotation and extension
- Cervical: stability - supports head
- Principle: loss of mobility at a mobile joint forces adjacent stability joint into mobility role

## Proximal Stability and Distal Mobility

- Core stability: lumbopelvic-hip complex provides stable base for limb function
- Timing: core activates before limb movement - feedforward stabilization
- Transversus abdominis: first muscle to activate in anticipation of limb loading
- Intraabdominal pressure: diaphragm, pelvic floor, and abdominal wall create pressure cylinder
- Distal efficiency: proximal stability allows distal segments to move freely and powerfully
- TCM parallel: Dan Tian lower energy center as proximal stability foundation for all movement

## Movement Screening

- FMS: Functional Movement Screen - 7 fundamental patterns scored 0-3
- Patterns: deep squat, hurdle step, inline lunge, shoulder mobility, active straight leg raise, trunk stability, rotary stability
- Asymmetry: left-right asymmetry more predictive of injury than overall score
- Corrective hierarchy: correct mobility first, then motor control, then strength
- Guardian Interface: movement pattern assessment as somatic state input
