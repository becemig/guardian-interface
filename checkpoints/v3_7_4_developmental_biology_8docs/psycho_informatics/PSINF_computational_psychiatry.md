# Computational Psychiatry

## Abstract

Computational psychiatry — the application of mathematical and computational models to the understanding, diagnosis, and treatment of psychiatric disorders — represents one of the most significant theoretical advances in mental health science of the early 21st century, offering a principled framework for understanding how disordered brain function produces the diverse symptom profiles of psychiatric conditions. The Bayesian brain hypothesis proposes that the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine — a hierarchical generative model that continuously generates predictions about the causes of sensory signals, compares those predictions to incoming sensory data, and updates its internal models to minimize prediction error. Karl Friston's free energy principle extends this Bayesian framework into a unified theory of biological self-organization: living systems minimize free energy — a quantity that bounds surprise — by either updating internal models to better predict the world or by acting on the world to make it conform to predictions. Active inference describes the bidirectional process by which the brain both updates beliefs to reduce prediction error and generates actions to fulfill predictions — collapsing the distinction between perception and action into a single inference process. Markov blankets — the statistical boundaries that separate a system's internal states from its external environment, mediated by active and sensory states — provide the formal description of the boundary between the brain's generative model and the world it models. Psychiatric disorder as inference failure reframes conditions like depression, anxiety, psychosis, and addiction as specific patterns of miscalibrated precision weighting — the brain's assignment of confidence to different levels of its predictive hierarchy. Interoceptive inference — the application of predictive processing to the body's internal signals — provides the bridge between computational psychiatry and somatic medicine, describing how disordered interoceptive prediction produces the somatic symptoms of psychiatric conditions and how somatic practices that improve interoceptive signal quality can recalibrate psychiatric inference.

## Predictive Processing

- The brain generates top-down predictions about sensory causes at every level of the hierarchy.
- Bottom-up sensory signals carry prediction errors — the difference between predicted and actual input.
- Learning updates the generative model to reduce future prediction errors.
- Perception is controlled hallucination — the brain's best guess about the causes of sensory signals.

## Precision Weighting

- Precision is the brain's confidence in a prediction or prediction error signal.
- High precision: the signal is trusted and drives strong model updates.
- Low precision: the signal is discounted and drives weak model updates.
- Psychiatric disorders represent specific patterns of precision miscalibration across the predictive hierarchy.

## Disorder as Inference Failure

- Depression: excessive precision on negative prior beliefs — the world will be bad — suppressing updating.
- Anxiety: excessive precision on threat prediction errors — hypervigilance to potential danger signals.
- Psychosis: excessive precision on bottom-up prediction errors — aberrant salience of random signals.
- Addiction: hijacked precision weighting — the substance acquires excessive precision as a predicted reward.
