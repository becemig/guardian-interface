# Self-Organized Criticality

## Abstract

Self-organized criticality SOC — the theoretical framework introduced by Per Bak, Chao Tang, and Kurt Wiesenfeld in 1987 — proposes that many complex systems in nature spontaneously evolve toward a critical state poised between order and disorder — the edge of chaos — from which they produce scale-free avalanche dynamics characterized by power law distributions that span many orders of magnitude. The sandpile model — grains of sand added one at a time to a pile produce avalanches of all sizes following a power law distribution with no characteristic scale — is the canonical SOC model that demonstrates how a simple local rule adding one grain at a time produces global critical behavior without any tuning of parameters. Power laws — mathematical relationships in which the frequency of an event scales as the inverse power of its size, producing straight lines on log-log plots with no characteristic scale — are the signature of SOC dynamics and are found throughout biology, neuroscience, and medicine: in the size distribution of neural avalanches in cortical networks, in the fluctuations of heart rate and other physiological signals, in the distribution of earthquake magnitudes, and in the statistics of species extinction. Neural avalanches — the propagating cascades of neural activity observed in cortical slice preparations and in vivo recordings — follow power law size distributions with an exponent of negative three halves, the theoretical prediction for a critical branching process. Branching ratio sigma — the average number of neurons activated by each active neuron in a neural cascade — is the key order parameter for brain criticality: a branching ratio of exactly one produces critical dynamics with power law avalanches; below one the activity dies out subcritically; above one the activity explodes supercritically. The edge of chaos — the critical point between ordered and chaotic dynamics — is proposed to be the dynamical regime where complex systems including the brain achieve maximum computational capacity, information transmission, and adaptive flexibility. The TCM cultivation ideal of dynamic balance — neither deficient nor excessive, neither too still nor too active — corresponds precisely to the SOC critical state as the optimal dynamical regime for biological function.
