# Microbiome Diet and Prebiotics

## Abstract

Microbiome diet and prebiotics — the profound influence of dietary composition on gut microbiome diversity, composition, and metabolic function, with diet being the primary modifiable determinant of gut microbiome composition in adults, and with the shift from traditional high-fiber plant-diverse diets toward the Western diet characterized by low fiber, high animal protein, refined carbohydrates, and processed foods producing the progressive loss of microbiome diversity that may underlie the epidemic of inflammatory and metabolic diseases in industrialized populations. Dietary fiber and microbiome diversity — Justin Sonnenburg's research demonstrating that dietary fiber is the primary fuel for beneficial gut bacteria, with reduced fiber intake causing the gut microbiome to degrade the intestinal mucus layer for energy leading to barrier dysfunction and inflammation, and with the diversity of plant foods consumed being more predictive of microbiome diversity than any other single dietary factor — the American Gut Project finding that consuming more than 30 different plant foods per week was associated with significantly greater microbiome diversity than consuming fewer than 10. Fermented food clinical trial — the 2021 Sonnenburg and Gardner Stanford RCT demonstrating that a diet high in fermented foods including yogurt, kefir, fermented cottage cheese, kimchi, vegetable brine drinks, and kombucha for ten weeks produced greater increases in microbiome diversity and greater decreases in inflammatory markers including 19 cytokines than a high-fiber diet in healthy adults — establishing fermented foods as a powerful microbiome intervention with immunological effects. Prebiotic classification and evidence — the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics definition of prebiotics as substrates selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring health benefits, with the confirmed prebiotics including inulin and fructooligosaccharides from chicory and Jerusalem artichoke, galactooligosaccharides from human milk and supplemental sources, and resistant starch from cooled cooked potatoes and legumes, each with specific bifidogenic and health effects documented in clinical trials. The Five Grain Spleen nourishment microbiome correspondence in TCM describes the classical importance of the five grains as the foundational foods nourishing the Spleen and providing the Post-Heaven Qi — a classical nutritional framework corresponding to the modern evidence that whole grain resistant starch and diverse plant fiber are the primary nutritional substrates maintaining gut microbiome diversity and the SCFA production sustaining colonic health.

## Fiber and Diversity

- 30 plants per week: significantly higher microbiome diversity — American Gut Project.
- Fiber types: inulin pectin resistant starch beta-glucan — different bacterial communities.
- Mucus degradation: low fiber causes microbiome to degrade protective mucus layer.
- Western diet: low fiber high fat protein — reduced diversity associated with disease.

## Fermented Food RCT

- 10 weeks fermented food: greater diversity increase than high fiber diet.
- 19 cytokines: decreased including IL-6 IL-12 IL-17 — reduced inflammation.
- Species increase: fermented food group gained more novel microbiome species.
- Mechanism: live bacteria from fermented foods — direct microbiome seeding.

## Prebiotic Evidence

- Inulin FOS: chicory Jerusalem artichoke — bifidobacterium selective.
- GOS: galactooligosaccharides — human milk prebiotic — bifidobacterium infant.
- Resistant starch: cooled cooked starches — butyrate producer fuel.
- Clinical effects: improved stool frequency reduced inflammation immune effects.
