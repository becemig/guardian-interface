# TCM Five Element Nutrition

## Abstract

TCM dietary therapy applies the five element correspondence system to food selection — organizing foods by their flavor, color, temperature, and energetic action to selectively nourish specific organ networks, address pattern imbalances, and support seasonal energetic transitions. The five flavor correspondences are the foundation of TCM dietary therapy: sour flavor enters and nourishes the Liver, bitter flavor enters and nourishes the Heart, sweet flavor enters and nourishes the Spleen, pungent or acrid flavor enters and nourishes the Lung, and salty flavor enters and nourishes the Kidney. These are not absolute rules but clinical guidelines — small amounts of the flavor nourish the corresponding organ while excess of any flavor damages it. The pharmacological basis of flavor-organ correspondence is emerging through modern research: the sour astringent quality of organic acids in liver-supporting foods directly corresponds to the astringent contraction that consolidates Liver Yin; the bitter alkaloids in Heart-supporting foods produce the cardiovascular and neurological effects corresponding to Heart network tonification. Seasonal eating in TCM aligns food choices with the energetic transitions of the five seasons: spring corresponds to Wood-Liver and emphasizes fresh green sprouting foods that support Liver Qi rising; summer corresponds to Fire-Heart and emphasizes light cooling foods that support Heart clarity; late summer corresponds to Earth-Spleen and emphasizes warming nurturing foods that support Spleen transformation; autumn corresponds to Metal-Lung and emphasizes pungent consolidating foods that support Lung Wei Qi; winter corresponds to Water-Kidney and emphasizes warming concentrated foods that support Kidney Yang and preserve constitutional Jing. Color correspondences align with the organ network system: green foods for Liver, red foods for Heart, yellow-orange foods for Spleen, white foods for Lung, black and dark foods for Kidney.

## Five Flavors and Organ Entry

- Sour: fermented foods, citrus, vinegar — enters Liver, astringes, consolidates Yin and fluids.
- Bitter: dark greens, coffee, dark chocolate — enters Heart, drains Heat, dries Dampness.
- Sweet: grains, root vegetables, most cooked foods — enters Spleen, tonifies Qi, nourishes Blood.
- Pungent: onions, ginger, garlic, spices — enters Lung, disperses, moves Qi and Blood.
- Salty: seaweed, miso, sea salt — enters Kidney, softens hardness, descends and moistens.

## Seasonal Eating

- Spring Wood: emphasize sprouted grains, fresh greens, light sour foods supporting Liver Qi rise.
- Summer Fire: emphasize bitter greens, cooling fruits, light easily digestible foods supporting Heart.
- Late Summer Earth: emphasize cooked sweet root vegetables, warming congees supporting Spleen transformation.
- Autumn Metal: emphasize pungent aromatic foods, white foods, consolidating preparations supporting Lung Wei Qi.
- Winter Water: emphasize warming soups, black beans, walnuts, concentrated preparations supporting Kidney Yang.

## Color and Organ Correspondences

- Green foods: chlorophyll-rich vegetables directly support Liver detoxification and Blood nourishment.
- Red foods: lycopene and anthocyanin-rich foods support Heart circulation and Blood quality.
- Yellow-orange: carotenoid-rich foods support Spleen digestive function and immune regulation.
- White foods: white mushrooms, daikon, pears support Lung moisture and Wei Qi.
- Black and dark: black sesame, black beans, dark seaweed support Kidney Jing and constitutional vitality.

## TCM Correspondence

- Flavor-organ entry corresponds to the pharmacological selectivity of specific phytochemicals for specific organ systems.
- Seasonal eating alignment corresponds to the TCM principle of following natural Qi transitions rather than opposing them.
- Color-organ correspondence corresponds to the phytochemical concentrations in foods that support corresponding organ functions.
- Dietary therapy as preventive medicine corresponds to the TCM principle that the superior physician prevents disease through lifestyle guidance.
