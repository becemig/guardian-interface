# Light as Medicine History

## Abstract

The history of light as medicine — spanning from the ancient Egyptian Greek and Indian heliotherapy traditions through Niels Finsen's Nobel Prize-winning phototherapy for lupus vulgaris, Auguste Rollier's heliotherapy sanatoriums for tuberculosis, the photodynamic therapy revolution of the twentieth century, and the contemporary renaissance of photobiomodulation, light therapy for depression, and transcranial photobiomodulation — demonstrates that the therapeutic use of light is one of the oldest and most cross-culturally validated medical traditions, now finding rigorous mechanistic validation through the modern understanding of photobiology. Heliotherapy — the systematic therapeutic use of sunlight, practiced in ancient Egypt where sun temples served as healing centers, in ancient Greece where Hippocrates recommended sunbathing at Cos, in ancient India in the Ayurvedic tradition, and revived in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries by Niels Finsen and Auguste Rollier — represents the foundational light medicine tradition, validated by the discovery of solar UV radiation's antibacterial and immunomodulatory effects and by the modern epidemiological evidence for the health benefits of adequate sun exposure. Niels Finsen — the Danish physician awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1903 for his development of phototherapy using concentrated ultraviolet radiation for the treatment of lupus vulgaris cutaneous tuberculosis — established light therapy as a scientifically validated medical intervention and founded the field of photobiology, demonstrating for the first time with rigorous clinical evidence that specific wavelengths of light have specific therapeutic effects on human disease. Photodynamic therapy PDT — the modern cancer and skin disease treatment combining photosensitizing drugs that accumulate preferentially in tumor tissue with visible light activation to produce cytotoxic singlet oxygen — represents the convergence of photobiology and pharmacology, with its roots in the ancient Indian and Egyptian use of psoralen-containing plants combined with sunlight for skin conditions now validated as psoralen-UVA PUVA therapy for psoriasis. Neonatal jaundice phototherapy — the treatment of neonatal hyperbilirubinemia with blue light at 460-490 nm that photoisomerizes bilirubin to water-soluble forms that can be excreted without liver conjugation — was discovered serendipitously when nurses noted that jaundiced infants near windows cleared faster, and has since become one of the most widely applied phototherapy treatments in medicine. Color therapy traditions — the use of specific wavelengths and colors for therapeutic purposes in Ayurvedic chromotherapy, Chinese five-element color correspondences, Rudolf Steiner's anthroposophical color medicine, and Dinshah Ghadiali's spectro-chrome therapy — represent the pre-scientific intuitive systematization of spectral therapeutics that anticipated many of the wavelength-specific therapeutic effects now being validated in photobiomodulation research.

## Finsen and Phototherapy Origins

- 1893: Finsen demonstrates UV radiation from carbon arc lamp treats lupus vulgaris.
- 1903: Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for phototherapy.
- Mechanism: UV radiation kills Mycobacterium tuberculosis in skin lesions.
- Founded photobiology as scientific discipline — Copenhagen Light Institute.

## Rollier Heliotherapy

- 1903: Auguste Rollier opens heliotherapy sanatorium in Leysin Switzerland for tuberculosis.
- Systematic graduated sun exposure protocol building from feet upward over weeks.
- Clinical outcomes: documented recovery in pulmonary and bone tuberculosis patients.
- Pre-antibiotic era: heliotherapy one of few effective tuberculosis treatments.

## Photodynamic Therapy

- Ancient psoralen use: Ammi majus plant for vitiligo in Egypt India — psoralen plus sunlight.
- Modern PDT: photosensitizer accumulates in tumor then activated by visible light.
- Mechanism: singlet oxygen production causing oxidative cancer cell death.
- Applications: skin cancers esophageal cancer lung cancer macular degeneration.
