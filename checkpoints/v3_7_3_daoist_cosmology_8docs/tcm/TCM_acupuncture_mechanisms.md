# Acupuncture Mechanisms

## Abstract

Modern research has made significant progress in elucidating the physiological mechanisms of acupuncture beyond the classical Qi framework while also revealing biological substrates that correspond meaningfully to classical concepts. Helene Langevin's connective tissue research demonstrated that acupuncture points and meridians correspond to planes of loose connective tissue and fascial cleavage planes that extend throughout the body creating a continuous anatomical substrate for needle stimulation. Needle manipulation producing De Qi — the characteristic sensation of heaviness, distension, and aching — causes measurable winding of connective tissue around the needle with propagating mechanical signals through the fascial network activating mechanoreceptors and fibroblast signaling pathways. Nanna Goldman's research demonstrated that adenosine — released locally at acupuncture points during needling — mediates the local anti-nociceptive effects of acupuncture through A1 receptors. fMRI studies document acupuncture-induced deactivation of the default mode network and limbic system corresponding to the De Qi response contrasting with sham acupuncture. Point specificity research using fMRI demonstrates that different acupoints produce distinct brain activation patterns corresponding to their classical indications — ST-36 activates different regions than PC-6 or HT-7. Electroacupuncture at specific frequencies releases different neuropeptides as demonstrated by Han Jisheng providing frequency-dependent therapeutic control.

## Connective Tissue Signaling

- Acupuncture points correspond to locations where nerves and vessels penetrate the deep fascia — fascial cleavage planes.
- Needle rotation causes connective tissue winding creating mechanical tension propagating along fascial planes.
- This mechanical signal activates fibroblasts through mechanotransduction producing cytoskeletal changes and signaling molecule release.
- The fascial network provides a continuous anatomical substrate corresponding to the classical meridian pathway concept.

## Adenosine Mechanism

- Needle stimulation releases adenosine locally at the acupuncture point through tissue trauma and ATP breakdown.
- Adenosine activates A1 receptors on local sensory neurons producing anti-nociceptive effects.
- Blocking adenosine with caffeine or A1 receptor antagonists reduces acupuncture analgesia.
- Enhancing adenosine signaling with deoxycoformycin prolongs and enhances acupuncture analgesia.

## De Qi and fMRI

- De Qi sensation — heaviness, distension, aching, numbness — corresponds to activation of deep tissue mechanoreceptors.
- fMRI during De Qi shows deactivation of the default mode network and limbic system distinguishing it from sham.
- Different acupoints produce distinct fMRI activation patterns corresponding to their classical organ and meridian associations.
- Sham acupuncture at non-points produces different neural signatures than true point stimulation.

## TCM Correspondence

- De Qi as the arrival of Qi at the needle corresponds to measurable connective tissue, adenosine, and neural responses.
- Point specificity documented by fMRI corresponds to the classical precision of acupoint indication in TCM texts.
- Meridians as fascial planes corresponds to the classical description of meridians as pathways through the body.
- Electroacupuncture frequency-dependent neuropeptide release provides mechanistic support for classical tonification-sedation principles.
