# Fractal Geometry in Biological Systems

## Abstract

Fractal geometry — the mathematical framework for describing self-similar structures that repeat their pattern across multiple scales — was developed by Benoit Mandelbrot and provides the language for describing the characteristic architecture of biological systems: from the branching of the bronchial tree and vascular system to the folding of the cerebral cortex, from the branching of neurons to the distribution of collagen fibers in fascia, biological structures at every scale exhibit fractal self-similarity that reflects the developmental processes that generate them and the functional requirements they serve. A fractal is a structure with a non-integer Hausdorff dimension — a dimension between one and two for a fractal curve or between two and three for a fractal surface — that quantifies how completely the structure fills space at different scales. The bronchial tree of the lung — branching from the trachea through 23 generations of progressive bifurcation to the terminal alveoli — is a near-perfect fractal with a dimension of approximately 1.7, achieving the maximum surface area for gas exchange within the constrained volume of the thorax through fractal space-filling. The vascular system — branching from the aorta through arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules, and veins back to the vena cava — follows Murray's law of optimal branching that minimizes the total work required to maintain blood flow through the fractal vascular tree. The cerebral cortex — whose characteristic folding pattern of gyri and sulci increases surface area threefold within the constrained volume of the skull — has a fractal dimension that decreases in certain neurological conditions, providing a biomarker of cortical development and neurodegeneration. Collagen fibers in the fascial system exhibit fractal organization across scales from the molecular to the gross anatomical, corresponding to the multi-scale mechanical properties of fascia and the fractal self-similarity of the meridian pathway maps. The fractal architecture of the body provides the structural basis for the scale-free dynamics observed in physiological signals — fractal structure produces fractal dynamics.
