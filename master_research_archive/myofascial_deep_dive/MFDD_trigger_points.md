# Trigger Point Neuroscience

## Abstract

Trigger point neuroscience — the scientific investigation of the nature, mechanism, and clinical significance of myofascial trigger points — the hyperirritable spots within a taut band of skeletal muscle that are painful on compression and produce characteristic referred pain, tenderness, and autonomic phenomena — has generated both a robust clinical framework through the work of Travell and Simons and a mechanistic understanding through the integrated hypothesis of David Simons and the biochemical characterization of trigger point microenvironments by Jay Shah, corresponding to the TCM concept of Ashi points as sites of Qi stagnation and Blood stasis that respond to needling. The integrated hypothesis of trigger point formation — Simons' proposal that trigger points arise from dysfunctional motor end plates in which excessive acetylcholine release from nerve terminals produces sustained depolarization of the motor end plate, generating the sustained contracture of a local group of sarcomeres that creates the palpable nodule and taut band — is supported by the finding that trigger points are located at motor end plate zones, that end plate noise abnormal spontaneous electrical activity is present at trigger point sites, and that the local twitch response produced by needling represents the involuntary spinal cord reflex contraction of the trigger point nodule. Biochemical characterization of trigger point microenvironments — Shah's microdialysis studies demonstrating that active trigger points have significantly elevated concentrations of substance P, CGRP, bradykinin, serotonin, norepinephrine, tumor necrosis factor alpha, interleukin-1 beta, and protons corresponding to reduced pH, compared to both latent trigger points and normal muscle — provides the chemical basis for the tenderness, referred pain, and autonomic phenomena of active trigger points through the sensitization of local nociceptors by the inflammatory soup of algogenic substances. Referred pain patterns — the characteristic maps of referred pain produced by each trigger point that Travell and Simons documented empirically for virtually every muscle in the body — overlap significantly with both dermatomal pain patterns and with TCM acupoint-to-meridian referred sensation patterns, suggesting a common mechanism of convergent spinal sensitization underlying both traditional and clinical observation of pain referral. The Ashi point Qi stagnation nodule correspondence in TCM describes the classical understanding of Ashi points — tender points that arise spontaneously at sites of Qi stagnation and Blood stasis and that respond to needling — as the traditional clinical recognition of what modern science identifies as myofascial trigger points.

## Integrated Hypothesis

- Excessive ACh release at dysfunctional motor end plate.
- Sustained end plate depolarization producing local sarcomere contracture.
- End plate noise: spontaneous electrical activity measurable at trigger point site.
- Energy crisis: contracted sarcomeres compress local circulation producing ischemia and metabolite accumulation.

## Biochemical Microenvironment

- Active trigger points: elevated SP CGRP bradykinin serotonin NE TNF-alpha IL-1beta.
- Reduced pH: acidic microenvironment activates ASIC acid-sensing ion channels.
- Latent trigger points: less elevated biochemicals — less pain but similar taut band.
- Treatment effect: dry needling normalizes biochemical microenvironment.

## Referred Pain Mechanism

- Local sensitization: algogenic soup sensitizes local nociceptors.
- Spinal convergence: nociceptive input from trigger point sensitizes dorsal horn neurons.
- Referred pain: sensitized dorsal horn neurons mislocalize pain to convergent dermatome.
- Central sensitization: chronic active trigger points contribute to widespread pain sensitization.

## TCM Correspondence

- Ashi points correspond directly to myofascial trigger points as sites of Qi-Blood stagnation.
- Needling producing local twitch response corresponds to the De Qi response at Ashi points.
- Trigger point referred pain patterns overlap with meridian pathway referred sensation.
- Dry needling and acupuncture needling produce similar biochemical normalization at trigger point sites.
