# Narrative Therapy

## Abstract

Narrative therapy — developed by Michael White and David Epston in Australia and New Zealand during the 1980s — is a postmodern psychotherapeutic approach that understands people's lives and identities as constituted through the stories they tell about themselves and their experience, and that therapeutic change occurs through the reauthoring of those stories. The foundational premise of narrative therapy is that the person is not the problem — the problem is the problem — and that by externalizing the problem giving it a separate name and identity outside the person, the person can develop a different relationship to the problem and discover their own capacities and values in resisting or managing it. Every person's life contains more events and experiences than any single story can capture — the dominant story that organizes the person's understanding of their life inevitably leaves out vast amounts of experience that does not fit its plot. Unique outcomes are the events and experiences that contradict the dominant problem story — moments of competence, resistance, connection, or meaning that exist in every person's life but have been overlooked because they don't fit the problem-saturated narrative. By identifying unique outcomes and building alternative stories around them, narrative therapy helps people develop counter-narratives that provide a richer, more complex, and more empowering account of their lives. Witnessing practices — where the person's emerging alternative story is witnessed by an audience who reflect back what they heard and its significance for them — amplify the social dimension of narrative change, grounding the new story in relational acknowledgment. Re-membering conversations address the relational nature of identity — the people who have contributed to the person's sense of who they are are members of a life club that can be consciously curated, strengthening connections to figures who support the preferred identity and revising connections to those who contributed to the problem story. Shen story reauthoring corresponds to the TCM understanding that the Heart Shen is the narrator of the life story, and that healing the Shen involves reauthoring the dominant story from one of depletion and defeat to one of cultivation and meaning.

## Externalizing

- The problem is given a name separate from the person — Anxiety, The Fog, The Critic.
- Externalizing questions explore the problem's influence: when does it show up, what does it tell you?
- Counter-influence questions explore the person's influence on the problem: when have you stood up to it?
- Externalizing creates the space between person and problem where agency and alternative action become possible.

## Unique Outcomes

- Unique outcomes are events that contradict the problem story — moments when the problem did not dominate.
- They exist in every person's life but have been overlooked because they don't fit the dominant narrative.
- Building an alternative story from unique outcomes creates a counter-narrative of competence and resilience.
- This corresponds to the TCM practice of finding and amplifying the existing healthy Qi amid the pathological pattern.

## Witnessing

- Outsider witness groups reflect back what they heard in the person's story and its significance.
- Witnessing grounds alternative stories in social acknowledgment amplifying their reality and durability.
- The witnessed story becomes more real through relational acknowledgment — identity is inherently relational.
- This corresponds to the TCM lineage transmission context where the teacher witnesses and affirms the student's development.

## TCM Correspondence

- Alternative narrative building corresponds to the TCM cultivation practice of progressively developing the healthy Shen amid pathology.
