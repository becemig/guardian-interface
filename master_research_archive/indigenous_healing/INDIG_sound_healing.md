# Indigenous Sound Healing Traditions

## Abstract

Sound is the oldest therapeutic technology in the human record. Before the needle, before the herb, before the knife — there was the voice, the drum, the rattle, and the song. Indigenous healing traditions across every continent have developed sophisticated systems of therapeutic sound use whose underlying mechanisms are increasingly validated by neuroscience and psychoacoustics. The shamanic drum — typically tuned to 4-7 Hz beat frequency — entrains the brain into theta-dominant states that open access to subcortical emotional memory, exactly as modern neurofeedback does deliberately. Icaros — the medicine songs of Amazonian vegetalismo — are not decorative accompaniment to the plant medicine ceremony: they are the primary therapeutic technology, encoding diagnostic information, intention, and healing power in melodic and rhythmic structures transmitted from plant spirits during dieta. The Aboriginal songlines of Australia represent perhaps the most extraordinary integration of sound and landscape in human culture: an acoustic map of the entire continent encoded in song, where singing the correct song in the correct location activates the healing power of that place in the living landscape. Tibetan singing bowls, Himalayan overtone chanting, and the throat singing of Tuvan and Mongolian traditions all exploit the acoustic physics of overtone series to produce complex frequency mixtures that activate multiple neural resonance frequencies simultaneously. The neuroscience is now established: rhythmic auditory stimulation synchronizes neural oscillations, reduces cortisol, increases oxytocin, activates the default mode network, and modulates pain through endogenous opioid release.
